## Prologomena

My first ordinary differential equations (ODE) course was strange. Not because it was unusual, but because it wasn’t. At this point, after taking several undergraduate math courses, that had trained me to think in terms of mathematical objects, structures, and proofs, differential equations felt like a return to something more mechanical. I could solve many of the assigned problems, but I rarely felt like there was any "aha!" moments or deeper insights gained when solving these equations, or why differential equations were supposed to be difficult to solve in the first place.

Instead of treating linear ordinary differential equations as what can appear as a disjointed a bag of tricks, I want to look at five less conventional ways of solving them.

## An Overview of Standard methods to solve ODEs

Before we get into that, let's give a brief overview of the methods one would normally encounter in an initial course on ODEs. Which will later
provide points of contrast with the methods we'll later reveal and the pedagogical improvements they provide over the standard methods. To that end, consider
a second order ODE of the form:

$$ a(x)\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} + b(x)\frac{dy}{dx} + c(x)y = 0 $$

where $a(x),b(x),c(x) \in \mathbb{R}$, and $y\in \mathbb{C}^\infty(\mathbb{R})$, in other words, $a(x), b(x)$ and $c(x)$ are real-valued functions and
$y$ is a function that can be differentiated an infinite number of times. If $a(x) = a, b(x) = b $ and $ c(x) = c$ for some $a,b,c \in\mathbb{R}$, that is, they are all constant functions, eyeballing the above equation, we may notice that if
$y(x)$ were $e^{kx}$ for some $k\in\mathbb{C}$ then this would reduce to

$$ ak^2 + bk + c = 0,$$

which is just a quadratic equation in $k$. And the method of finding the solution for this kind of ODE is called the **method of constant coefficients**. The form (and character?) of the solutions depend on which of the three cases they boil down to: If the roots of the quadratic equation are real and distinct, let's call them $k_1$ and $k_2$, then the solutions are $e^{k_1x}$ and $e^{k_2x}$; if there is a repeated root, $k$, then the solution is $e^{kx}$ and $xe^{kx}$; and if the roots are complex then the solutions take the form $ C_1e^{kx}$ and $C_2e^{\overline{k}}x$ where $k = w + zi$ for $w, z\in\mathbb{R}$ and $\overline{k} = w - zi$ is the complex conjugate of $k$.

Now that the homogeneous, constant-coefficient case is settled, the natural move is to loosen our assumptions one at a time and see how far the same guess-and-check philosophy carries us.

**First loosening: keep the coefficients constant, but let the equation be inhomogeneous.** Suppose $a,b,c$ are still constants, but now

$$ a\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} + b\frac{dy}{dx} + cy = f(x), \qquad f(x)\not\equiv 0.$$

The general solution splits as $y = y_h + y_p$, where $y_h$ is exactly the homogeneous solution from before, and $y_p$ is any single solution that accounts for $f(x)$. Finding $y_p$ is where a new ansatz enters: the **method of undetermined coefficients**. The idea is that if $f(x)$ is a polynomial, an exponential, a sine or cosine, or some product of these, then $y_p$ should have that _same flavor_ — a polynomial of matching degree, or $Ae^{\alpha x}$, or $A\cos(\beta x) + B\sin(\beta x)$, and so on — with the coefficients $A, B, \dots$ left undetermined. Plugging this guess back into the ODE turns the problem into a system of linear equations for those coefficients. It's efficient, but only as good as your table of "recognizable" $f(x)$'s — feed it $f(x) = \sec(x)$ or $f(x) = 1/x$ and it has nothing to offer.

That gap is what motivates **variation of parameters**. Instead of guessing the shape of $y_p$ from $f(x)$, we lean on the two homogeneous solutions $y_1, y_2$ we already have and _promote_ the constants multiplying them to unknown functions:

$$ y*p(x) = u_1(x)y_1(x) + u_2(x)y_2(x).$$

Substituting back (with a convenient constraint to keep things linear) reduces the problem to solving for $u_1'(x)$ and $u_2'(x)$ via the Wronskian of $y_1, y_2$, then integrating. This works for _any_ $f(x)$, and — this is the key generalization — it doesn't actually need $a,b,c$ to be constant at all. As long as you know two linearly independent solutions $y_1(x), y_2(x)$ of the homogeneous equation $a(x)y'' + b(x)y' + c(x)y=0$, variation of parameters hands you $y_p$ for the inhomogeneous version. The catch, of course, is that "as long as you know $y_1, y_2$" — for general $a(x),b(x),c(x)$, finding those in the first place is its own problem.

**Second loosening: let the coefficients vary, but in a very particular way.** One case where we _can_ find $y_1, y_2$ outright is the **Euler (or Cauchy–Euler) equation**,

$$ ax^2\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} + bx\frac{dy}{dx} + cy = 0,$$

where $a,b,c$ are constants but each derivative is weighted by a matching power of $x$. The trick here is a different ansatz: try $y(x) = x^r$ for some $r \in \mathbb{C}$. Substituting gives

$$ a\,r(r-1) + br + c = 0,$$

again just a quadratic — this time in $r$ rather than $k$ — and the same three cases resurface: real distinct roots $r_1, r_2$ give $x^{r_1}, x^{r_2}$; a repeated root $r$ gives $x^r, x^r\ln x$; and complex roots $r = w \pm zi$ give $x^w\cos(z\ln x), x^w\sin(z\ln x)$. It's the constant-coefficient story wearing a different costume — swap $x = e^t$ and the Euler equation _becomes_ a constant-coefficient one in $t$.

Laid side by side, the pattern is hard to miss:

| Method                    | Equation form                                        | Ansatz                      | Reduces to                     | Solution form                                       |
| ------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------- | ------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------- |
| Constant coefficients     | $a y'' + b y' + c y=0$                                   | $y=e^{kx}$                  | $ak^2+bk+c=0$                  | $e^{k_1x}, e^{k_2x}$ (or repeated/complex variants) |
| Undetermined coefficients | $a y'' + b y' + c y=f(x)$, $f$ of "recognizable" form | $y_p \sim f(x)$             | linear system for coefficients | $y = y_h + y_p$                                     |
| Variation of parameters   | $a(x)y'' + b(x)y' + c(x)y=f(x)$, $y_1,y_2$ known   | $y_p = u_1(x)y_1+u_2(x)y_2$ | $u_1', u_2'$ via Wronskian     | $y = y_h + y_p$ (integral form)                     |
| Euler–Cauchy              | $ax^2y^{''}+bxy^{'}+cy=0$                            | $y=x^r$                     | $ar(r-1)+br+c=0$               | $x^{r_1}, x^{r_2}$ (or repeated/complex variants)   |

Four methods, four equation forms, four different letters for the unknown — but notice they're all doing the same move.

The common thread in the aforementioned techniques is that we all assummed a solution and plugged in that guess or ansatz first then tried to derive some relation based off of that. This way of going about things is unenlightening for many reasons:

1. it only equips us with cookie cutter reciepes for a very specific set of equations and are useless for any differential equations that we may encounter in the wild that don't follow this format;
2. It reduces much of the subject to a game of pattern matching: identify the type of equation, remember the corresponding ansatz, and apply the associated recipe. The emphasis shifts from understanding the underlying structure of the equation to recognising which trick happens to work.
3. it lacks any abstractions or generalisability.

## Some notes on quasi-exact solvability

Every method we've discussed so far shares a limitation: you either guess the exact form of $y(x)$ and get lucky (constant coefficients, Euler equations), or you fall back on the general integral formula from variation of parameters, which is exact but often useless in practice because you can't evaluate the integrals in closed form.

It also doesn't get across the point that some differential equations are seemingly intractable. It would be a great point to demonstrate why it is so difficult to solve differential equations in general and what we can salvage from that. Instead of thinking of solving an ODE in terms of putting all the pieces of a jigsaw puzzle together, it may be the case that there are pieces missing and we have to use whatever pieces we have to help us get an idea of how the overall puzzle looks like. Up until now we have been able to solve a differential equation completely meaning there is no restriction on the solutions in terms of the domain in which they are valid or for particular choices of the parameter. However, differential equations in real life are more about tradeoffs, and how we go about finding what those tradeoffs are. 

 There's a third option that sits between these — one that doesn't try to solve the whole equation, but instead asks a narrower, cleverer question:

> Instead of solving the differential equation, can we find a _finite-dimensional space_ that the differential operator maps into itself?

If the answer is yes, then a piece of the problem — sometimes all of it, sometimes just part of the spectrum — collapses into ordinary linear algebra. This is the idea behind **quasi-exact solvability (QES)**, and it rests on a hidden Lie algebra sitting inside the differential operator.

### Building the algebra

Consider three differential operators acting on functions of $x$:

$$ J_{+} = x^2\frac{d}{dx} - jx, \qquad J_0 = x\frac{d}{dx} - \frac{j}{2}, \qquad J_{-} = \frac{d}{dx}, $$

where $j_2$ is a fixed parameter we'll pin down shortly. A direct computation shows these satisfy

$$ [J_+, J_-] = -2J_0, \qquad [J_0, J_\pm] = \pm J_{\pm}, $$

which are exactly the commutation relations of $\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{R})$. So we've smuggled a piece of Lie algebra into calculus. The payoff comes from how these operators act on monomials $x^m$:

| Operator | Action on $x^m$                  | Effect                     |
| -------- | -------------------------------- | -------------------------- |
| $J_-$    | $mx^{m-1}$                       | lowers degree by 1         |
| $J_0$    | $\left(m-\tfrac{j}{2}\right)x^m$ | preserves degree, diagonal |
| $J_+$    | $(m-j)x^{m+1}$                   | raises degree by 1         |

So $J_{-}$ and $J_{+}$ behave like annihilation and creation operators from quantum mechanics, and $J_0$ just reads off the degree. The interesting case is $J_+$: it raises degree _except_ when $m=j$, where the coefficient $(m-j)$ vanishes and it kills the monomial outright:
$$ J_{+} x^j = 0. $$

That single vanishing is the entire mechanism behind QES. It means the space

$$ \mathcal{P}_j = \text{span}\{1, x, x^2, \dots, x^j\} $$

is _closed_ under all three generators — $J_-$ and $J_0$ can't leave it (they never raise degree), and $J_+$ can't either, because it's forbidden from stepping past $x^j$. Without that truncation, $J_+$ would push degree up forever and no finite space would survive.

### From algebra to matrices

Here's where it connects back to our running theme. Suppose the differential operator you actually care about, call it $H$, can be written as a polynomial in $J_+, J_0, J_-$ — say $H = aJ_+J_- + bJ_0 + cJ_+ + dJ_- + e$. Since each generator preserves $\mathcal{P}_j$, so does any combination of them:

$$ H: \mathcal{P}_j \to \mathcal{P}_j. $$

Now the ansatz is no longer "guess $y=e^{kx}$" or "guess $y=x^r$" — it's "restrict attention to a polynomial $y(x) = \sum_{n=0}^j a_n x^n$." Because $\mathcal{P}_j$ is finite-dimensional, substituting this ansatz turns the differential equation $Hy = Ey$ into an honest matrix eigenvalue problem $Ma = Ea$, solvable by computing $\det(M - EI) = 0$.

A quick example makes this concrete. Take $H = J_+J_-$ with $j=2$, so the basis is $\{1, x, x^2\}$. Acting term by term: $H(1) = 0$, $H(x) = -2x$, and $H(x^2) = -2x^2$. In this basis, $H$ is simply the diagonal matrix

$$ H = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -2 \end{pmatrix}. $$

The differential equation has become a matrix diagonalization — no integration required.

### Why "quasi"

The word "quasi" is doing real work here. Compare two situations:

- **Exact solvability**: the operator preserves an _entire infinite tower_ of nested spaces $\mathcal{P}_0 \subset \mathcal{P}_1 \subset \mathcal{P}_2 \subset \cdots$. Every polynomial sector closes, so the whole spectrum is algebraically accessible. This is the situation for the classical special-function equations — Hermite, Laguerre, Legendre, hypergeometric — which is no accident, since those equations are themselves built from $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ raising/lowering structure.
- **Quasi-exact solvability**: only _one_ finite space $\mathcal{P}_j$ is preserved, for a specific $j$ fixed by the equation's parameters. Finitely many eigenstates are obtainable exactly; the rest of the spectrum genuinely requires numerics. This is generically what happens for more complicated potentials — double wells, sextic oscillators, anharmonic oscillators — governed by Heun-type equations rather than hypergeometric ones.

The connection to physics comes through a gauge transformation: writing $\psi(x) = e^{-W(x)}P(x)$ and choosing $W(x)$ to absorb the equation's asymptotic behavior, the Schrödinger operator acting on the leftover polynomial piece $P(x)$ becomes exactly an $\mathfrak{sl}_2$- type operator like the $H$ above. 

Exact solvability then corresponds to eliminating all uncontrolled _degree-raising_ terms in the operator; quasi-exact solvability corresponds to eliminating the uncontrolled _degree-lowering_ terms instead, while still permitting a raising operator that terminates at one highest state $x^j$ — precisely the highest-weight condition $J_+x^j=0$ from a finite-dimensional representation of $\mathfrak{sl}_2$.

|                     | Exact solvability                                              | Quasi-exact solvability              |
| ------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| Invariant structure | infinite flag $\mathcal{P}_0\subset\mathcal{P}_1\subset\cdots$ | single space $\mathcal{P}_j$         |
| Parameter condition | no net degree-raising terms                                    | raising terminates only at $x^j$     |
| Spectrum obtained   | entire spectrum, algebraically                                 | finitely many states; rest numerical |

## The Asymptotic Iteration Method

Let's set up the equation the way we have throughout: starting from the general form $a(x)y''+b(x)y'+c(x)y=0$ and dividing through by the leading coefficient $a(x)$, we can always write a second order linear ODE in the normalised form

$$ y'' = p(x)y' + q(x)y, \tag{1} $$

where $y'$ denotes differentiation with respect to $x$, and $p(x), q(x) \in C^\infty(a,b)$ — that is, $p$ and $q$ are infinitely differentiable on the open interval $(a,b)$. Nothing new so far; this is just our usual equation with the sign convention flipped so that $y''$ sits by itself on the left.

Now here's the trick: differentiate $(1)$ once more with respect to $x$, and whenever a $y''$ term shows up on the right, substitute $(1)$ back in for it. Doing so gives

$$ y''' = p' y' + p\,y'' + q' y + q y' = p'y' + p(py'+qy) + q'y + qy', $$

which tidies up to

$$ y''' = p_1 y' + q_1 y, \qquad p_1 = p_0' + p_0^2 + q_0, \quad q_1 = p_0 q_0 + q_0', \tag{2} $$

with $p_0 = p_0(x) = p(x)$ and $q_0 = q_0(x) = q(x)$. Notice that we've traded a second-derivative relation for a *third*-derivative relation, still written entirely in terms of $y'$ and $y$ — no $y''$ survives the substitution.

There's nothing stopping us from repeating this indefinitely. Differentiating $(2)$ and substituting $(1)$ back in wherever $y''$ appears, and continuing inductively, gives the general relation for the $(n+2)^{\text{th}}$ derivative:

$$ y^{(n+2)} = p_n y' + q_n y, \qquad n = 0, 1, 2, \dots \tag{3} $$

with the coefficients satisfying the recursion

$$ p_{n+1} = p_n' + p_0 p_n + q_n, \qquad q_{n+1} = q_0 p_n + q_n'. \tag{4} $$

So every derivative of $y$, no matter how high, reduces back down to a linear combination of just $y'$ and $y$ — the price we pay is that the coefficients $p_n, q_n$ themselves obey an ever-more-complicated recursive system.

Now take the ratio of consecutive derivatives in this family:

$$ \frac{y^{(n+3)}}{y^{(n+2)}} = \frac{p_{n+1}y' + q_{n+1}y}{p_n y' + q_n y}. \tag{5} $$

Here's where the method makes its leap. We *require* that, for sufficiently large $n$, this ratio settles down to a limit that no longer depends on $n$:

$$ \lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{y^{(n+2)}}{y^{(n+1)}} = L(x). \tag{6} $$

The idea is that if every derivative of $y$ eventually scales by the same factor $L(x)$, then $y$ itself must behave locally like $e^{\int L\,dx}$ — the one function whose derivatives all pick up the same multiplicative factor. Feeding this ansatz back into the recursion $(4)$ and matching the leading behavior as $n\to\infty$ forces $L$ to satisfy

$$ L' = p_0 L + q_0 - L^2, \tag{7} $$

a Riccati equation — the very same Riccati equation you'd get from the standard substitution $w = y'/y$ applied directly to $(1)$, just arrived at by a much longer road.

For constant coefficients $p_0 = p$, $q_0 = q$, equation $(7)$ is autonomous and separable. Writing the right-hand side in terms of the roots $k_1, k_2$ of $k^2 - pk - q = 0$ — yes, the *same* characteristic equation from our very first method — we can factor $q + pL - L^2 = -(L-k_1)(L-k_2)$ and integrate by partial fractions to get

$$ \frac{L(x) - k_1}{L(x) - k_2} = C_1 e^{(k_1-k_2)x}, \qquad C_1 \in \mathbb{R}, \tag{8} $$

and the right-hand side follows from $(7)$ since separating variables gives

$$ \int \frac{dL}{(L-k_1)(L-k_2)} = -\int dx \;\implies\; \frac{1}{k_1-k_2}\ln\left|\frac{L-k_1}{L-k_2}\right| = -x + \text{const}, $$

which rearranges into $(8)$.

Replacing this expression for $L$ back on the right-hand side of $y'/y = L(x)$ gives us a first-order differential equation for $y$ itself,

$$ \frac{y'}{y} = L(x), $$

whose solution yields the solution of $(1)$ as

$$ y(x) = C_2 \exp\left(\int^x L(s)\,ds\right), \qquad C_2 \in \mathbb{R}. $$

Set $C_1 = 0$ in $(8)$ and you get $L \equiv k_1$ identically, recovering $y = C_2 e^{k_1 x}$ — exactly one of the two exponential solutions from the method of constant coefficients. Letting $C_1 \to \infty$ instead recovers $k_2$. So this elaborate detour through infinitely many derivatives eventually lands us back at the same answer we got in two lines from the ansatz $y = e^{kx}$.

This method amazingly produces the solutions for a differential equation with constant coefficients, as well as some differential equations with variable coefficients. I say "amazingly" because the derivation lacks rigour. The way this result is "proven" is very hand-wavy, and that's how it's presented in the seminal paper it first appeared in. The convergence condition $(6)$ is also badly understated: what kind of convergence is being assumed — pointwise in $x$, uniform, $L^2$? And once granted, do we have any sense of the *rate* of convergence relative to other known methods, or is $(6)$ simply asserted and left unexamined?

This approach is worth presenting precisely because it invites deeper inquiry. It raises questions that compel us to examine the method more thoroughly, and its ability to reproduce some of the same solutions as conventional techniques is striking and deserves closer analysis. That said, it's important to note that the method can be misleading — it does not solve ordinary differential equations with constant coefficients in full generality, as is implicitly acknowledged in the original paper. In fact, the derivation above only ever recovers *one* root at a time, selected by the choice of $C_1$; nothing in the construction naturally hands you the full two-parameter general solution $C_ae^{k_1x}+C_be^{k_2x}$, and when the roots $k_1,k_2$ coincide or are complex conjugates — so that neither root dominates the other in magnitude — the limit in $(6)$ has no reason to exist at all, and the method simply has nothing to say.
